01

Objective

A professional profile is only useful if people actually fill it out and most people freeze in front of a blank 'tell us about yourself' field. Beyond that, OneTap needed profiles to work for completely different kinds of professionals: a COO, a freelance photographer, a startup mentor. The page itself had to be customizable, not just the copy.

02

03

The Research

DUAL-PERSPECTIVE MAPPING (FILLER VS. VIEWER) PROFESSIONAL ARCHETYPE ANALYSIS CONTENT AUDIT OF EXISTING PROFILE PRODUCTS ITERATIVE WIDGET PROTOTYPING
01

The filler and the viewer have opposite needs

The layout that makes the user's life easy — long free-text fields, space to say everything — is often the layout that fails the viewer. Nobody wants to read a wall of text to figure out if someone is credible. Every widget decision came back to this tension.

02

Blank page paralysis is the core UX barrier

'Describe your impact' is a terrible prompt. It asks someone to write from scratch in a format they haven't seen, for an audience they're not sure about, about achievements they're not sure how to frame. This isn't a motivation problem — it's a structure problem.

03

The viewer needs density control the user never thought to ask for

Users instinctively want to say everything. But viewers only need the headline until they decide to go deeper. The mismatch isn't the user's fault — the product wasn't giving them a way to show appropriate detail at first glance.

04

'Portfolio' means something different to every profession

A Behance grid is right for a designer. A YouTube thumbnail strip is right for an educator. A case study link list is right for a strategist. Forcing everyone into one preview format would flatten most professions' best work into something that looked out of place.

04

The Solution

Each widget type came directly from one of the tensions in the research. Impact Cards solve blank-page paralysis. Compact and expanded variants solve the density mismatch. The timeline view of experience solves the inference problem (what does this person's career trajectory actually look like?). Portfolio format previews solve the profession-specificity problem. None of these were invented for completeness. Each one removed a specific failure mode.

WHAT IT DOES
✦

Impact Cards: sentence templates ('Increased ___ by ___ at ___') + shuffle for different phrasing — number-first format, user never writes from scratch

✦

Compact / expanded variants for Bio, Prizes, Impact — user chooses density, viewer gets the right level of detail at first glance

✦

Experience as a timeline — shows progression and overlap, not just a flat list of titles

✦

Featured portfolio: Behance-style, YouTube-style, or a running list — format matched to profession, not forced into one mould

✦

Drag-and-drop widget ordering — the COO and the photographer control their own hierarchy

✦

Shuffle mechanic on Impact Cards — changes the sentence frame, breaks the blank-page freeze without adding a decision

05
HIGHLIGHT OF THE PROJECT

"The templates and shuffle mechanic turned out to be the most important piece of the whole system — because they removed the decision rather than adding one."

It would've been easy to give users more flexibility: a richer text editor, more formatting options, longer fields. But every extra option is a decision the user has to make, and each one is a chance to lose them. The shuffle mechanic cost almost nothing to design. It turned out to be the feature that got the most done, because it answered the question the user was actually stuck on: what do I even write?

07

What I Learned

01

Customization isn't 'give users more options'

Every extra option is a decision the user has to make, and each one is a chance to lose them. The templates and shuffle mechanic worked because they removed the decision rather than adding one. The best customization systems make one good choice easy — not every possible choice available.

02

The viewer's ease is the binding constraint

It's easy to design for the person filling out a form — they're present, they have opinions, they give feedback. The viewer is invisible. Every widget I built needed to answer the viewer's question ('is this person worth my time?') in under three seconds, or the whole profile failed regardless of how good the content was.

03

The best variants weren't the most flexible

The widget variants that worked weren't the ones that gave users the most control. They were the ones that made the viewer's judgment easier while asking the user to do less thinking. Constraint, applied in the right place, is what made the system feel effortless rather than limited.
